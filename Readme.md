# Quality Quest
## A friendly guide for NCAT · ISEN 425 · Quality Engineering

**Open `Quality_Quest.html` and press “Start with Q.” That is all you need to begin.**

Quality Quest is a learning game about checking and improving quality. You will inspect candy bags, investigate a scanner’s warnings, prepare a rover, test a robot, improve a filling line, and check clinic supply kits.

You do not need to know the formulas before you play. Q, the small robot guide, shows you an example first. Then you try a small task yourself. After you see what happens, the game connects the result to an idea from class.

**The aim is to understand why something happens.** A badge means you completed a guided mission and explained its main idea. You are not racing anyone. There is no countdown, and a wrong answer does not take away points or lives.

## How this connects to ISEN 425

Quality engineering asks whether a product or process meets its requirements, how often it misses them, and what changes might improve it.

The game helps you answer three useful questions:

1. **How likely is a problem?** You will see the difference between one item failing and a whole batch raising an alert.
2. **What does a warning mean?** You will learn why a scanner’s flag can come from an item that is actually good.
3. **What should we improve?** You will see why a good average is not enough when individual measurements vary.

The first three missions use the class probability problems. The next three apply related ideas to robotics, manufacturing, and healthcare supply inspection. The instructor section explains the exact source used for the class problems.

## Know what to do on each screen

**Read the task beside Q. Then follow the line that begins “Do this now.”**

The game only asks you to handle the current step. You do not need to plan the whole mission.

| What you see | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Watch Q** | Press the gold play button. Watch an item move through the check station. |
| **Two items to choose from** | Read their labels, then tap or click one item. |
| **Answer choices** | Choose one answer. Read the feedback before moving on. |
| **A slider and a target** | Press **Find the slider** if you need help locating it. Move it toward the target. Use **−** or **+** for smaller, precise moves. |

The gold button stays at the bottom during a mission. Its words change to match the next action. For example, it might say **Play the example**, **Test this setting**, or **Collect my discovery badge**.

Sometimes you need to choose an item or answer before you can continue. In that case, the gold button says **Choose an item in the picture**, **Choose my guess**, or **Choose my answer**. Pressing it takes you to the choices. **It does not choose an answer for you or skip the task.**

On a small screen, the picture may be below Q’s instructions. You can use **Show me the picture ↓** to reach it and **Back to my task ↑** to return. When you start an animation, the game brings the moving scene into view.

### If you are unsure

Press **Help me with this step**. Q gives a short hint for your current task.

You can also open **Help** at the top. It shows where you are, explains the controls, and contains a small word guide. Q’s explanations are built into the game. You do not need to type a question or use an online AI service.

## Your first mission: a complete walkthrough

This walkthrough uses **The candy launch**. Follow it while you play if you want someone to walk you through every stage.

There are **11 small steps** in each mission. Some steps contain a few short cards or a second experiment. You can stop between steps and come back later when browser storage is available.

### Step 1 · Understand your job

Your job is to help the packing team decide whether a batch of candy bags can leave.

A bag marked **Low** contains too little candy. The class calls this **underweight**.

Read that one idea, then press **Show me one item**.

### Step 2 · Watch Q check one bag

Press **Play the example**.

Watch the bag travel into the check station. The station checks it, and the label changes to **× Low**. The checked bag then appears in the results tray.

You do not have to calculate anything. Just notice what the label means.

When the example ends, press **Let me try**. You can also use **Replay this example** if you want to watch again.

### Step 3 · Find the bag that needs attention

Two bags appear. One is marked **✓ OK**. The other is marked **× Low**.

Tap the **Low** bag. Read the explanation. If you choose the other bag, the game gives you a clue and lets you try again.

Then press **Now show me a whole group**.

### Step 4 · Watch a 12-bag check

The team checks 12 bags together. Its rule is:

> Hold the batch when **2 or more bags are underweight**.

Press **Play the group**. The bags arrive one at a time. Count the Low bags as they appear in the tray.

This teaching example contains two Low bags. You can pause to count them. After the last bag arrives, the results stay on the screen.

Press **Let me make the decision**.

### Step 5 · Make the batch decision

The example had two Low bags. Choose whether to hold the batch or let it leave.

Read the feedback. The phrase **“2 or more” includes 2**, so the rule tells the team to hold this batch.

Press **Show me the class setup**.

### Step 6 · Learn the class settings

Q introduces the settings one card at a time:

- One check contains **12 bags**.
- Each bag has an **8% chance of being underweight**.

The picture with 100 small squares helps explain 8%. About 8 out of 100 bags are underweight in the long run. It does not mean every group of 100 must contain exactly eight Low bags.

Use **Show me the next idea** to move through the cards. On the last card, press **I’m ready to make a guess**.

### Step 7 · Make a guess and run the experiment

Q asks how many checks out of 100 you think will raise an alert.

Choose the answer that feels closest. **Your guess does not have to be correct.** It gives you something to compare with the result.

Press **Run 1,000 checks**.

The experiment now creates random outcomes. The counter grows toward 1,000. Here, **one check means a whole group of 12 bags**, so 1,000 checks involve 12,000 bags.

The moving scene also shows recent actual outcomes. Those recent items are only a small window into the experiment. The counters include all completed checks.

### Step 8 · Read the result as a fraction

Q asks for the whole group used in the calculation.

We are asking about **checks that raise an alert**, so the whole group is **all 1,000 checks**. We are not calculating the percentage of individual bags that are Low.

Once you choose the whole group, the game shows:

> Checks with an alert ÷ all checks × 100

For example, if a run produced 247 alerts, its result would be 247 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 24.7%. Your run may produce a different count.

The class model predicts about **24.9%**. This is different from the **8%** chance for a single bag. The question changed from one bag to a whole 12-bag check.

Read the comparison, then press **Now change one thing**.

### Step 9 · Improve the packing line

The slider controls the chance that one bag is underweight. Press **Find the slider** if it is below the instructions.

1. Move it from **8%** to **2%**. You can drag the slider or press **−** six times.
2. Watch the three groups change: **0 Low**, **1 Low**, and **2+ Low**.
3. Notice that the alert rule stays the same. Two or more Low bags still trigger an alert.
4. Press **Test this setting**.
5. Compare the changed experiment with the changed model prediction.

Fewer bags are underweight, so fewer checks raise an alert. The chance is smaller, but it is not zero.

After testing the target setting, press **Explain what you discovered**.

### Step 10 · Explain why the result changed

Choose the explanation that matches the change you made.

The packing line improved because **each bag became less likely to be underweight**. The rule did not change, and the improvement did not guarantee that every batch would pass.

A wrong explanation gives a specific clue. Read it and try again.

Then press **Collect my discovery badge**.

### Step 11 · Keep your discovery

You earned the **Chance Explorer** badge. The recap shows the original and changed model predictions beside each other.

You can now:

- Write a note in **My discovery**.
- Open **Class problem, step by step** to connect the experiment to the homework calculations.
- Choose **Try my own settings** for more experiments.
- Choose **Try a new example** to use the idea in a different question.
- Use **Save my notebook** to keep a separate copy of your work.
- Follow the gold button to the next unfinished mission.

A useful note is:

> I changed the underweight chance from 8% to 2%. Alerts became less common because fewer bags were Low. One bag’s chance and a whole batch’s chance are different.

Write it in your own words. It does not need to sound formal.

## Understand the numbers and pictures

### A practice example teaches the rule

The first one-item and group demonstrations have **planned outcomes**. They are labeled **PRACTICE EXAMPLE**.

For example, the first candy group always contains two Low bags. This makes the alert rule easy to see. Replaying it repeats the same example.

**Do not use this small planned example to estimate the real probability.** Its purpose is to help you understand what counts as a pass, a failure, or a flag.

### A random experiment shows possible outcomes

The later 1,000-trial experiment generates random outcomes from the settings you learned.

One run can differ from another. That is normal. A longer experiment can make a pattern easier to see, but it does not have to match the prediction exactly.

The live counters are actual counts from that run. The recent-item display also uses actual sampled outcomes. It shows the latest batch, rover, or robot check, or the most recent individual toys, bottles, or kits.

### A model predicts a long-run chance

**Class model predicts** is the mathematical prediction for the current settings. It uses assumptions such as independent outcomes or a normal distribution of measurements.

The model is not the same thing as the most recent random result. When you change a setting, the model prediction updates. The game then asks you to run a fresh experiment at those settings.

Compare like with like: the original model with the changed model, and each random experiment with the model for its own settings.

### “Out of 100” does not mean the app ran only 100 trials

A percent expresses a result as an amount out of 100.

The app runs 1,000 trials, then converts the result into a percentage. This makes different experiments easier to compare. In the scanner missions, the percentage uses the flagged group, which is smaller than the total number of inspected items.

### Know what one trial means

| Mission | One trial is… | The main result asks… |
|---|---|---|
| Candy | One check of 12 bags | What fraction of checks raise an alert? |
| Scanner | One inspected toy | Among the flagged toys, what fraction are defective? |
| Rover | One set of 8 bearings | What fraction of rovers get all 8 bearings that fit? |
| Robotics | One robot startup | What fraction of startups have every required actuator working? |
| Manufacturing | One filled bottle | What fraction of bottles meet the fill limits? |
| Healthcare | One inspected supply kit | Among the flagged kits, what fraction are incomplete? |

### Read the measurement pictures

The bearing and bottle lessons show a fixed green band. This band represents the allowed measurements.

The **average** marker shows the center of the process. Moving the average shifts the example measurements sideways. Reducing the **spread** moves them closer together.

A dot inside the limits passes. A cross outside the limits misses the requirement. Labels and symbols explain the result, so color is not the only clue.

These 12 moving measurement points are **illustrative examples**. They help you see the effect of changing the settings. Their count is not the probability calculation. The separately labeled model prediction gives that probability.

### Why some scanner counts have decimals

An actual experiment counts whole toys or kits. You cannot inspect 0.2 of a toy.

A model may predict **28.2 true flags per 1,000 toys**. This is an average across many possible groups. Some groups will have more true flags and some will have fewer.

The game labels these values as **expected counts**. It labels the simulated counts as actual results.

## The six missions

Start with candy if you are new. After that, you can follow the suggested order or choose a mission from **Missions**. Each uses the same small-step pattern, so you do not have to learn a new set of controls.

### 1. The candy launch · Binomial probability

**Your job:** decide when a 12-bag check should raise an alert.

You begin with an 8% underweight chance for each bag. Two or more Low bags trigger an alert. You then improve the packing process by reducing the underweight chance to 2%.

**Watch for:** the difference between one bag’s chance and a whole check’s chance.

**Keep this idea:** a small chance for one item can produce a different chance when several items are combined under a rule.

**Optional new example:** explain why an average count of 0.96 does not require each check to contain exactly 0.96 Low bags.

### 2. The suspicious scanner · Conditional probability

**Your job:** work out what a scanner’s flag really means.

Only 3% of toys are defective. The scanner catches 94% of defective toys. It also flags 7% of good toys by mistake.

1. Watch a good toy receive a false flag.
2. Identify a false flag yourself.
3. Open a tiny group containing one true flag and two false flags.
4. Run the random experiment.
5. Choose **all flags** as the whole group for the percentage.
6. Reduce **Good toys flagged by mistake** to **1%** and test it.

**Watch for:** the true and false flags. Both belong in the flagged group.

**Keep this idea:** “the scanner catches 94% of defects” does not mean “94% of its flags are defects.” The two statements start with different groups.

**Optional new example:** use four true flags and six false flags to calculate the share of flags that found defects.

### 3. Ready, set, rover · Normal variation and assembly yield

**Your job:** prepare a rover whose eight bearings all fit.

A bearing must measure **24.90–25.10 mm**. The starting average is **25.00 mm**, with a standard deviation of **0.06 mm**.

1. Watch a bearing that is too large fail its size check.
2. Pick a bearing that fits.
3. See why a set with seven fitting bearings and one oversized bearing cannot pass.
4. Run 1,000 rover trials.
5. Use **all 1,000 rovers** as the whole group.
6. Move the average to **25.05 mm** and test the drift.

**Watch for:** the example sizes move, but the allowed limits stay fixed.

**Keep this idea:** a single bearing fits about 90% of the time, while only about 45% of complete eight-bearing sets pass at the original settings. A good average does not guarantee that every part fits.

**Optional new example:** find the chance that two independent parts both fit when each has a 90% pass chance.

### 4. Robot power-up · Reliability

**Your job:** help a robot pass its startup check.

Each actuator moves a robot joint. Each has a 2% chance of failing, and every required actuator must work.

1. Learn the difference between a working and failed joint.
2. See how one failed required joint stops the practice robot.
3. Run the original six-actuator robot.
4. Change the number of actuators from **6** to **12**.
5. Run the new robot and explain what changed.

**Watch for:** adding required actuators gives the robot more chances to fail, even though each actuator’s failure chance stays the same.

**Keep this idea:** multiplying individual pass chances requires an independence assumption. A shared power failure can affect several joints together.

**Optional new example:** decide whether independent-joint calculations fully describe a robot whose joints all share one battery.

### 5. The perfect pour · Process centering and variation

**Your job:** improve a filling line until the model predicts that at least 99% of bottles meet the requirement.

Customers accept **490–510 mL** in each bottle. The original process averages **504 mL**, with a spread of **7 mL**.

This mission makes two changes in order:

1. Move **Average fill** to **500 mL**.
2. Press **Test this setting**. Centering helps, but predicted yield is still only about 85%.
3. Press **Next: reduce the spread**.
4. Keep the average at 500 mL. Move the spread to **3.5 mL or less**.
5. Test again. At 3.5 mL, the model predicts about **99.57%** acceptable bottles.
6. Explain why centering alone was not enough.

**Watch for:** moving the center and reducing the spread change the picture in different ways.

**Keep this idea:** the requirement applies to individual bottles. A good average can hide bottles that are too empty or too full.

The goal requires a completed **1,000-bottle experiment** at settings with **at least 99% model yield**. A lucky random sample cannot replace the process-improvement requirement.

**Optional new example:** compare two centered filling lines that have different spreads.

### 6. Care kit check · Rare problems and false alarms

**Your job:** reduce unnecessary supply-kit rechecks in a fictional clinic.

Only 1% of kits are incomplete. The scanner catches 95% of incomplete kits and mistakenly flags 5% of complete kits.

1. Watch a complete kit receive a false flag.
2. Separate true and false flags in the practice example.
3. Run the random experiment.
4. Use **all flagged kits** as the whole group.
5. Reduce **Complete kits flagged by mistake** to **0.5%** and test it.

**Watch for:** false flags can outnumber true flags when incomplete kits are rare. Reducing false flags changes the composition of the flagged group.

**Keep this idea:** a flag is a reason to recheck. It is not proof that a kit is incomplete. Improving the scanner does not add missing items to kits.

This is a supply-inspection teaching example with fictional rates. It is not a patient-diagnosis tool.

**Optional new example:** consider how a flag’s meaning changes if incomplete kits become more common while scanner performance stays the same.

## Pause, replay, and move at your own pace

| Control | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Pause** | The animation stops. The current position, checked items, and counts remain. |
| **Resume** | The same run continues from its paused position. |
| **Replay this example** | The planned teaching example plays again. |
| **Stop this run** | The unfinished run is discarded. It does not replace an earlier completed result. |
| **Help → Use a slower pace** | One-item and group demonstrations move more slowly. The experiment counter also progresses more slowly. |
| **Help → Less motion** | Results appear without animated travel. The calculations and learning tasks remain the same. |
| **Help → Sound off / Sound on** | Switches optional short completion sounds off or on. |
| **Back** | Revisits the previous small step. Within the class-setup cards, it returns to the previous card. |

Opening Help or switching to another browser tab pauses a running animation. When you return, press **Resume**.

The game also respects your device’s reduced-motion preference. If that preference is on, switching the game’s own Less motion option off will not override the device preference.

Keyboard users can press **Tab** to move between controls, **Enter** or **Space** to activate buttons, and the **arrow keys** to adjust sliders. **Escape** closes a dialog. The − / + buttons are another way to make precise adjustments.

## Save your work

The app saves completed steps, guesses, results, notes, badges, and settings in the same browser when browser storage is available.

- If it opens at your current lesson, continue from there.
- If it opens at the introduction, choose **Continue with Q**. The introduction names the next unfinished mission.

1. Open **Notebook** at the top, **My notebook** inside Help, or **Save my notebook** after a badge.
2. Review the text.
3. Press **Download notebook**.
4. Save **Quality_Quest_My_Notebook.md** somewhere you can find it.

### A few saving details

Browser storage is local to that browser and device. It does not synchronize across computers. Private-browsing sessions may discard it when they close. Clearing browser data can remove it. Moving or renaming a local HTML file may also change how its saved data is accessed.

If browser saving is unavailable, the app tells you to keep a notebook copy. You can still play during the current session.

**Before switching devices or clearing browser data, download or copy your notebook.** The notebook is a readable record, not a file that can be imported to restore the game’s progress automatically.

### Restart a lesson without losing your notes

Open **Help → Restart this mission**. Read the confirmation, then choose **Restart the lesson**.

Only that lesson returns to step 1. Its written note, earned badge, and optional new-example result remain. Other missions keep their place.

## Small word guide

| Word | Easy meaning |
|---|---|
| Requirement | The condition an item must meet, such as a size or fill range. |
| Defect | A problem that makes an item fail its requirement. |
| Underweight | Containing too little material, such as too little candy in a bag. |
| Probability | How likely something is to happen. |
| Percent | An amount out of 100. |
| Trial | One repeat of an experiment. |
| Simulation | A computer experiment that creates possible outcomes under a model. |
| Model | A mathematical description used to predict what may happen. |
| Expected count | The average count across many possible repeats. It can have a decimal. |
| Flag | A warning that an item should be rechecked. |
| True flag | A flag that points to a real problem. |
| False flag | A flag on an item that is actually good. |
| Catch rate | The fraction of genuinely defective or incomplete items that receive flags. |
| Average or mean | Add the measurements and divide by the number of measurements. |
| Variation | Differences between individual measurements or outcomes. |
| Standard deviation | A measure of spread around the average. Larger values mean more variation. |
| Yield | The fraction that passes the stated requirement. |
| Independent outcomes | One outcome does not change the chance of another. |
| Binomial probability | A model for counting how many events occur in a fixed number of independent trials with the same event chance. |
| Conditional probability | A probability calculated within a specified group, such as items that received a flag. |

## Instructor guide

### Source and homework alignment

The first three missions follow the three quality adventures in **ISEN425_Probability_Review.tex**, dated **August 28, 2026**: gummy-bag inspection, the inspector bot, and Mars rover bearing fit. The app includes all **14 worked subparts** from those three problems.

A separately titled **Homework 1** handout has not been verified. Confirm this mapping before assigning the app specifically as Homework 1. The three application scenarios use fictional teaching settings.

### Suggested classroom use

Start by projecting the first one-bag candy example. Ask students what **Low** means before showing a formula. Then play the 12-bag example and ask them to explain the batch rule in words.

Let students work at their own pace. In pairs, one student can operate the controls while the other explains what happened. Have them swap roles for the next mission.

Start with one mission if class time is limited. Pause between missions to discuss the takeaway. The app has no fixed completion time, so allow for reading, replay, and discussion.

A simple submission can contain:

1. The downloaded notebook.
2. One sentence about what the student changed.
3. One sentence about what happened and why.

For example: “I kept the average at 500 mL and reduced spread. More bottles passed because individual fills stayed closer to the center.”

### Check understanding beyond following hints

Ask students to try the optional new-example question before opening its hint. Then give a fresh question in class or in your normal assessment system.

Useful prompts include:

| Ask students | Look for this understanding |
|---|---|
| What is one trial in the candy experiment? | One 12-bag check, not one bag. |
| Why is an 8% item probability different from the alert probability? | The alert concerns a count of two or more Low bags in a group. |
| Which total belongs below the number of true flags? | All flags, including false flags. |
| Does a mean inside the limits guarantee acceptable parts? | No. Individual measurements still vary. |
| Why can the rover’s complete-set yield be much lower than one bearing’s yield? | All eight required bearings must fit. |
| What could violate the robot model’s independence assumption? | A shared battery, controller, or other common failure cause. |
| Why did manufacturing need two changes? | Centering changes the average. Reducing spread changes consistency. |
| Does better inspection repair the underlying item? | No. Inspection measures or flags the output. |

Badges and XP represent guided completion. They are not grades, credentials, or evidence of independent mastery. The optional check also allows hints and retries. No classroom study of enjoyment or learning gains has been conducted.

### Model assumptions

The binomial, rover-set, and robot calculations assume independent outcomes. The size and fill models assume normal distributions. Shared causes or a different measurement distribution could change the answers.

Scanner catch and false-flag rates can be varied separately in this app. Real scanner thresholds may trade these rates off. Expected counts can be fractional. Actual simulated counts are whole numbers. Displayed percentages are rounded.

The healthcare activity concerns supply-kit inspection with fictional numbers. It does not model patient care or provide clinical advice.

## Technical notes for someone editing the app

The deliverable remains one standalone HTML file, approximately **12.7 MB**, plus this README. The HTML contains inline CSS, JavaScript, exact SVG charts, and five embedded PNG artwork assets. It has no external runtime dependencies, analytics, or account system. The optional reading links need internet.

The app uses `requestAnimationFrame` for motion. Simulations generate all 1,000 trials and update counts in batches of 50. A saved deterministic random-generator state makes a run reproducible from the same starting state. Normal samples use a Box–Muller transform. The normal probability calculation uses a normal-CDF approximation.

When editing the HTML, keep the related content together:

| Code name | What it controls |
|---|---|
| `LABS` | Model settings and optional exploration presets. |
| `GUIDE` | Mission goals, target controls, explanations, and badges. |
| `TINY` | Planned examples and the small learning steps. |
| `MATH` | Worked class answers. |
| `TRANSFER` | The optional new-example questions. |
| `guidance`, `scene`, `previewPanel` | The instructions, demonstrations, and changing visual previews. |

This revision saves under `isen425_quality_quest_v5`. It can carry forward the prior `isen425_quality_quest_v4` record and import older notes from `isen425_quality_quest_v2`. It does not erase those older saved records.

If model settings change, review the explanations, expected answers, guided targets, and examples together. A changed number in the model may require a changed lesson as well.
